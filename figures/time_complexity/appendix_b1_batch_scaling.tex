\textbf{Batch-size scaling.}
Table~\ref{tab:hirid_batch} and Figure~\ref{fig:hirid_batch} sweep the batch size
$B$ from 4 to 256 on HiRID, the widest schema in our benchmark. What REBASE
transfers across sources is a fixed set of $K$ prototype graphs of $T$ nodes, and
the transfer is realized by an alignment against those prototypes. The alignment
is solved by a fixed number of sequential solver iterations, each consisting of
the two matrix products of Eq.~(7); with $K=T=12$ the operands are far below the
saturation point of the device, so wall-clock time is governed by the iteration
count rather than by the amount of work inside an iteration. The alignment
therefore behaves as a per-step cost that is nearly flat in $B$ until the
operands become large enough to matter: REBASE moves from 0.117 s/step at
$B{=}4$ to 0.298 s/step at $B{=}256$, a $2.5\times$ increase for a $64\times$
larger batch, and throughput rises from 34 to 859 rows/s over the same range.
The transformer baselines show the opposite profile — a negligible per-step term
and a cost essentially proportional to $B$ (TransTab $6.9\times$, CARTE
$5.4\times$ across the sweep) — so the gap closes as the batch grows. At $B{=}4$
REBASE is $7.8\times$ slower than TransTab; at $B{=}256$ it is $2.9\times$, and
within $1.8\times$ of CARTE. The regime in which the alignment overhead is
visible is the small-batch regime, which is not the regime in which these models
are trained.

\textbf{Why XTFormer is the outlier.}
XTFormer is the only baseline that exceeds our cost at every batch size beyond
16, reaching $0.486$ s/step and $16.46$ GB at $B{=}128$ — $2.6\times$ the time
and $4.6\times$ the memory of REBASE — and it grows $31\times$ across the sweep,
the steepest slope in the table. The reason is the object it transfers. XTFormer
composes a prediction from $n_f$ shared basis functions and adapts to a table by
positioning a coordinate in that basis; as noted in Section~2, the coordinate is
assigned to each feature independently. This has a cost consequence that mirrors
the modeling one. Because the coordinate is attached to a feature rather than to
a table, the basis cannot be collapsed into one shared map before the forward
pass: either the $n_f$ basis functions are evaluated separately and combined at
every feature token, or the collapse produces a distinct $d\times d$ map per
feature and the weight sharing along the sequence is lost. Under either reading
the token-wise part of a block costs $n_f$ times a standard transformer block, in
compute and in stored activations alike, and the official configuration uses
$n_f=4$. The coordinate positioning protocol compounds this. It fixes an order
over the columns and lets a feature token attend only to its predecessors, which
is what allows one trunk to accept schemas of arbitrary width, but it requires
the dense $p_m \times p_m$ score matrix and a mask of the same shape to be formed
for each of the $h$ attention heads; with $h=32$ at $d=256$ this single map holds
$B h p_m^2$ entries, 1.22 GB at $B{=}128$ and $p_m{=}283$, against 0.04 GB for the
single-head map REBASE forms at the same width. Fitting $a B p_m + b B p_m^2$ to
the measured memory across the six ICU sources at $B{=}128$ yields $R^2=1.00$
($R^2=0.995$ for time), and the quadratic term accounts for $79\%$ of the
16.46 GB at $p_m{=}283$: the cost is set by the masked attention over the full
column sequence, not by the tokenizer or the basis composition. Crucially, both
terms of that budget are per instance, so every term carries a factor of $B$ and
there is nothing to amortize; XTFormer's throughput saturates near 260 rows/s
beyond $B{=}16$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hirid_batch}c) while REBASE continues to improve.
Extrapolating the fit, a single 48 GB device is exhausted at $B \approx 373$ on
HiRID, whereas REBASE reaches $B{=}256$ at 6.97 GB. The contrast is structural:
REBASE pays once per step to align a $p_m \times T$ plan against a fixed basis of
graphs, whereas XTFormer re-derives a per-feature coordinate and a full
$p_m \times p_m$ interaction map for every instance in the batch.
